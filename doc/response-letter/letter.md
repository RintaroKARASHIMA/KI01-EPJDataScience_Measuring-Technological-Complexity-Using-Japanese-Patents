**POINT-BY-POINT RESPONSE TO REVIEWERS’ COMMENTS:**

Manuscript “Measuring Technological Complexity Using Japanese Patents” (Submission ID c2970727-07a5-4193-93d3-809ed46060d2). Page and line numbers cited in the responses refer to the line-numbered PDF of the revised manuscript.

**Reviewer 1:**

**General comment:**

**This manuscript studies technological complexity using Japanese patent data and proposes a corporation-level implementation of the Technological Complexity Index, with the goal of comparing technology classes more robustly than in standard regional analyses and of extending the framework to temporal and regional comparisons. The topic is timely and potentially relevant for both innovation studies and the economic complexity literature, and the shift to the corporate level is in principle interesting. However, I have substantial reservations about the paper's literature positioning, the interpretation of the main metric, and the strength of several empirical and methodological claims. In its current form, I do not think the evidence fully supports some of the paper's main conclusions. My overall recommendation is major revision.**

Response: We thank the reviewer for this candid assessment. The revision addresses all nine major comments and five minor comments below. In brief, we rewrote the literature review to sharpen the novelty claim, clarified the interpretation of TCI relative to ubiquity and corrected an interpretive error we found in the process, and added a quantitative test of the quadrant structure in Fig. 1B. We diagnosed the stability claim in Section 2.2 directly through a subsampling experiment showing that most of the apparent advantage is a node-count effect, withdrew the prefecture-to-prefecture comparison because it could not be defended against a placebo null, and replaced it with a new out-of-sample validation. The Discussion has been rewritten so that each contribution matches what the Results demonstrate, the forward-looking claims about prediction and policy have been softened, the treatment of corporate identity and firm survival has been added to the Methods, and the filtering strategy is now justified with the sensitivity analyses the reviewer requested.

**Major comments:**

**Major comment**

**1. The literature review does not yet position the paper convincingly with respect to the existing patent-complexity literature. In the Introduction (roughly lines 87-106) the manuscript frames the contribution as a move from regional aggregation to the corporate level, and the Abstract makes similarly broad novelty claims about stability, regional comparability, and policy relevance (roughly lines 28-45). However, the relevant literature is both broader and closer to this paper than the current discussion suggests. In particular, the manuscript should engage more substantively with the literature it already cites, especially Balland and Rigby's foundational paper on the geography of complex knowledge (currently reference [15] in the manuscript) and the firm-level application currently listed as reference [18]. In addition, there are recent and directly relevant papers that are not currently integrated into the framing, including work on urban patent-based fitness and complexity [1] and work comparing technological fitness and coherence across geographical scales [2]. More broadly, there is now a substantial adjacent literature using patent data together with complexity-related constructs such as fitness, coherence, relatedness, and spatial concentration, including work on corporate technological portfolios and firm-level technological diversification paths [3]. I therefore suggest substantially revising the literature review and sharpening the novelty claim. The paper should explain more explicitly how it differs from prior patent-complexity studies in terms of unit of analysis, metric, empirical question, and substantive contribution. At present, the manuscript risks overstating novelty and underspecifying its contribution relative to adjacent work. A clearer discussion of overlap and differences with the recent literature on patent complexity, coherent diversification, relatedness, and firm-level technological evolution would help readers understand whether this study provides a genuinely new methodological or empirical contribution, rather than primarily a change in aggregation level and national setting.**

Response: We agree that the original framing overstated novelty and underspecified the contribution. We have rewritten the literature review in the Introduction (pages 2-3, lines 74-113). The revised text now positions Balland and Rigby explicitly as the pioneering application of the method of reflections to patent data, and it integrates the three references suggested by the reviewer. We now cite Straccamore, Bruno, Monechi, and Loreto (Scientific Reports, 2023) on urban patent-based fitness and complexity, Straccamore, Bruno, and Tacchella (PLOS One, 2025) on technological fitness and coherence across geographical scales, and Pugliese, Napolitano, Zaccaria, and Pietronero (PLoS One, 2019) on coherent diversification in corporate technological portfolios. We also state explicitly that micro-level complexity measurement is not new in itself and that the existing firm-level TCI application to Korean corporations does not compare the corporate measurement with a regional counterpart.

Following the reviewer's suggestion, the Introduction now contains a paragraph that states the difference from prior work along exactly the four axes the reviewer named (page 3, lines 100-113). The unit of analysis is the corporation as the actor side of the bipartite network while the technology side remains identical. The metric is the standard TCI without any new index, which enables a like-for-like comparison. The empirical question concerns the measurement itself, namely whether differences between the two levels reflect the level or the number of actor nodes, and whether the corporate estimates carry information the regional estimates do not. The substantive contribution is a diagnosis of when corporate-level measurement helps and when it fails, together with an out-of-sample entry test.

**Major comment**

**8. The dataset spans 1981-2010, yet many corporations active at the beginning of the period may no longer exist at the end, and some may have undergone mergers, restructuring, or name changes. The analysis appears to treat corporations as stable nodes in the bipartite network, but the manuscript does not explain how corporate identity is harmonized over time. Response: We have added a full explanation to the Data subsection of the Methods (Section 4.1, second paragraph, page 13, lines 561-575). Assignee names are harmonized using the correspondence table of the NISTEP corporate name dictionary (version 2023_1). Each assignee identifier is resolved iteratively to its final successor entity, so that patents filed under predecessor names are attributed to the successor corporation, and the original name is retained when no successor exists. Orthographic variations are normalized in the same procedure. This harmonization reassigns 680,493 patents, corresponding to 21.34 per cent of all records. The revised text also notes two properties of the procedure. The dictionary reflects name changes that occurred after the study period, so activity is attributed to the surviving legal entity, and parent and subsidiary corporations are not consolidated, so each node represents a harmonized legal entity rather than a corporate group. As part of this revision, we also updated the name harmonization to the current release of the dictionary, which maps some assignees further along their succession chains, for example corporations renamed in holding-company transitions, and we rebuilt the dataset with this release applied consistently. The update changed the number of retained corporations from 1,939 in the submitted manuscript to 1,936 and accounts for the small shifts in the reported statistics relative to the submitted version, for example the consistency statistic gamma from 0.60 to 0.585. We verified that no conclusion changes under the updated data, and all numbers in the revised manuscript and in this letter refer to the updated data.

On survival, the revised Data subsection (Section 4.1, page 14, lines 613-619) reports that although 84.6 per cent of all assignees stop filing before fiscal year 2010, only 154 of the 1,936 retained corporations (8.0 per cent) do so. The top 3 per cent filter therefore selects corporations that remain active for most of the study period, and the corporate nodes can be regarded as largely stable economic actors rather than as a heterogeneous collection of historical assignee records. Section 2.2 has been aligned with this description where the rolling rankings are discussed (page 8, lines 350-353).

**9. It is not yet clear why the top 3% filter is necessary for the complexity analysis. Response: We carried out the checks the reviewer proposed. The results are documented in the new Methods subsection Robustness Procedures (Section 4.3, page 16, lines 709-731) and in Tables S1 and S2 (page 19), with a summary in the Data subsection (Section 4.1, page 14, lines 619-626).

First, on the reviewer's question of whether the RTA step already prunes the sample, the answer is that it does not. All 64,457 assignees remain after the RTA step, with a median presence degree of 1, a mean of 2.45, and 57.80 per cent of corporations connected to a single technology class. Second, we compared the top 3 per cent filter with the post-RTA pruning rule the reviewer suggested, namely removing corporations with a single link after the RTA step (Table S2). This result is reported in Table S2 (page 19) and in Section 4.3 (page 16, lines 723-731), and it is consistent with the node-count mechanism established in Section 2.2, because degree-one removal keeps far more corporations than the top 3 per cent filter.

Third, we state in Section 4.1 and Section 4.3 why we nevertheless keep the top 3 per cent filter as the main specification. The criterion is defined ex ante on raw patent output and does not condition on the structure of the RTA network from which TCI is computed, and the alternative rule would only widen the gap we report, so the main specification is conservative in this respect. The threshold sensitivity analysis requested by Reviewer 2 (Table S1) further shows that the TCI rankings themselves are robust to the threshold, with Spearman correlations of at least 0.90 in absolute value against the main specification. We also revised the caption of Fig. S3 during this work so that it accurately describes the regenerated figure (page 29).

**Major comment**

**1. The headline comparison sets 47 prefectures against 1,939 corporations. These differ in two respects at once: the level (region versus firm) and the number of actor nodes (47 versus 1,939, a factor of roughly 40). The mechanism the authors themselves describe is a mechanism of node count, not of the corporate level. To isolate the level from the count, the authors would need to hold node count approximately fixed and vary only the level, for example by subsampling the corporate network down to matched sizes. Response: We ran the subsampling experiment the reviewer specified, and the result confirms the reviewer's conjecture. The experiment and its conclusion are now reported in Section 2.2 (page 8, lines 334-348) and Panel D of Fig. 2 (page 25), with the procedure documented in Section 4.3, Robustness Procedures (page 16, lines 702-708).

Following the design in the review, we drew random subsamples of corporations of size N = 47, 100, 500, and 1,936, with 100 repetitions for each N, recomputed the RTA network and the full comparison for each draw, and summarized each draw by the rank-biserial gamma (Section 2.2, Panel D of Fig. 2, and the Methods subsection Robustness Procedures). At N = 47, the median gamma drops from 0.585 to 0.057, and the 5th to 95th percentile range spans minus 0.783 to 0.705, which includes negative values. The median gamma rises with N, reaching 0.116 at N = 100, 0.511 at N = 500, and 0.585 in the full network. The median absolute residual at N = 47 is 18.896, which is close to the regional baseline of 19.602, and falls to 6.657 in the full network. The full network contains 1,936 corporations rather than the 1,939 of the submitted manuscript, because the name harmonization was updated to the current release of the dictionary during the revision, as explained in our response to Reviewer 1 Major comment 8.

The manuscript now states the conclusion the reviewer anticipated. Most of the difference observed in the consistency comparison is driven by the number of actor nodes rather than by the corporate level as such, and the remaining level effect at matched counts is small and unstable in sign. The Abstract (page 1), the Introduction (page 3, lines 120-123), and the Discussion (page 10, lines 446-454) have been rewritten accordingly. The first contribution is now presented as a diagnosis, namely that the value of the corporate level in this comparison is that it makes a large actor set available from the same patent data, whereas regional aggregation in Japan is inherently limited to 47 prefectures. In view of this finding, we added the external validation described in the next response, and the revised paper rests its case for corporate-level measurement on that test alone.

We also considered disaggregating the regional side to a finer geographic unit instead of subsampling the corporate side. We did not pursue this, because prefecture attribution itself relies on the patent holder's registered address, and 6.46% of the retained corporations have addresses recorded under more than one prefecture across the study period, mainly at multi-site firms, where the records do not distinguish formal headquarters relocations from variation in the recorded address, so disaggregating further would compound rather than resolve this source of noise. The attribution rule and the 6.46% figure are now stated in the Methods subsection Data (Section 4.1, page 14, lines 599-603), and the reason for not pursuing finer disaggregation is stated in the limitations paragraph of the Discussion (Section 3, page 11, lines 484-487).

**Major comment**

**2. A measure can be perfectly stable and entirely uninformative. Smaller residuals between the Schmoch and IPC rankings are equally consistent with two readings: the corporate measure is more accurate, or the corporate measure over-smooths and is insensitive to genuine granular signal. The only way to break the tie is to show that the corporate TCI predicts something external that the regional TCI predicts less well. I regard a predictive or external-validation exercise not as a desirable extension but as a condition for the paper's main claim to be supported at all. I would want to see, at minimum, one out-of-sample task in which corporate and regional TCI are compared head to head on an outcome neither is constructed from.**

Response: We agree that this validation is a condition rather than an extension, and we have carried it out. The new Section 2.3, Regional Information and Out-of-Sample Validation (pages 8-10, lines 362-438), reports a head-to-head out-of-sample comparison on an outcome neither measure is constructed from, with procedures documented in the new Methods subsection External Validation Procedures (pages 16-17, lines 733-773).

The design is as follows. All measures are constructed from fiscal years 1981 to 2010 only, and outcomes are observed in 2011 to 2015. The outcome is the entry of a prefecture into a technology class, defined as a prefecture-technology pair without revealed advantage in the estimation period that acquires it in the outcome period. At the three-digit IPC level there are 4,185 candidate pairs and 366 entries (8.7 per cent). We estimate logistic regressions of entry on TCI with prefecture fixed effects, past RTA, and relatedness density as controls, where the density variable follows the product space literature and the identical density column enters both specifications, so the corporate and regional specifications differ only in the network on which TCI is measured.

When both TCI variables enter jointly at the three-digit IPC level, the corporate TCI remains a significant predictor of entry while the regional TCI does not. The standardized coefficient per one standard deviation of TCI is 0.2166 with a 95 per cent confidence interval of 0.0509 to 0.3823 (p = 0.0104) for the corporate measure, and minus 0.0938 (p = 0.237) for the regional measure. Relatedness density itself is a strong predictor, but including it reduces the corporate coefficient by only about five per cent, and the two variables are nearly orthogonal at this level (r = 0.023). The result is also robust to constructing the proximity matrix from either the corporate or the regional presence matrix (p = 0.0104 and p = 0.0112). At the Schmoch level of 35 classes, neither measure is significant when entered jointly (corporate p = 0.223 without and p = 0.266 with the density control, both now stated in Section 2.3, page 9, lines 408-413, so that the text and Panel A of Fig. 3 can be read together), so the information advantage of the corporate measure appears precisely at the finer granularity, which parallels the mechanism documented in Section 2.2. A complementary regression on the intensive margin, the log growth rate of technology patent shares, shows a significant corporate coefficient with a negative sign only when the density control is included, and we present the sign contrast between the two outcomes as an observation to be explained rather than as an established mechanism.

Section 2.3 and the Discussion state explicitly that this is an information test rather than a proposal of a prediction tool, because most of the variation in entry is explained by the controls and the gain in in-sample discrimination from adding TCI is marginal. Section 2.3 now reports the in-sample area under the ROC curve (page 10, lines 417-422), which rises only from 0.7974 with the controls alone to 0.7989 with the corporate TCI and 0.7975 with the regional TCI, consistent with the Methods subsection External Validation Procedures. What the test establishes is that the corporate TCI contains statistically identifiable information about subsequent regional technological entry that the regional TCI does not. We also report in Section 2.3 the negative result described in our response to Reviewer 1 point 5, namely that estimating TCI directly on prefecture-restricted networks degenerates and that the original prefecture comparison has been withdrawn. The Discussion presents the negative and the positive part together as the paper's answer to the regional question.

**Major comment**

**3. Retaining only the top 3% of corporations by patent output is defensible in isolation, but three problems follow. Response: We carried out all three checks.

First, the threshold sensitivity analysis is reported in Table S1 (page 19) and in the Methods subsection Robustness Procedures (page 16, lines 709-722). We recomputed the full analysis at the top 1, 3, 5, 10, and 100 per cent thresholds, corresponding to 647, 1,936, 3,232, 6,463, and 64,457 corporations. The corporate rankings themselves remain stable across thresholds. We also compared the filter with a post-RTA degree-one pruning rule, as described in our response to Reviewer 1 point 9, and we report that this alternative yields a larger gamma (0.962) than our main specification (0.585). The Methods state why the top 3 per cent filter is nevertheless retained, namely that it is defined ex ante on patent output rather than on the network from which TCI is computed, and that the alternative would only widen the reported gap, so the main specification is conservative.

Third, on the like-for-like question, we verified the inputs on the analysis side and confirmed that the regional TCI in the Fig. 2 comparison is like for like by construction rather than by a separate post hoc check. The regional and corporate TCI values are both generated from the same underlying aggregation step, which is filtered once by the top 3 per cent criterion, so the regional side necessarily uses the same filtered patent set as the corporate side, and this is now stated explicitly in Section 2.2 (page 8, lines 327-330). Table S2 (page 19) notes that the regional side uses the 47 prefectures in all pruning configurations.
