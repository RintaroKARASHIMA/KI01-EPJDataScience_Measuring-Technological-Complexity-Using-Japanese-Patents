Reviewer 1

This manuscript studies technological complexity using Japanese patent data and proposes a corporation-level implementation of the Technological Complexity Index, with the goal of comparing technology classes more robustly than in standard regional analyses and of extending the framework to temporal and regional comparisons. The topic is timely and potentially relevant for both innovation studies and the economic complexity literature, and the shift to the corporate level is in principle interesting. However, I have substantial reservations about the paper’s literature positioning, the interpretation of the main metric, and the strength of several empirical and methodological claims. In its current form, I do not think the evidence fully supports some of the paper’s main conclusions. My overall recommendation is major revision.

Major comments

1. Literature positioning and novelty

The literature review does not yet position the paper convincingly with respect to the existing patent-complexity literature. In the Introduction (roughly lines 87-106) the manuscript frames the contribution as a move from regional aggregation to the corporate level, and the Abstract makes similarly broad novelty claims about stability, regional comparability, and policy relevance (roughly lines 28-45). However, the relevant literature is both broader and closer to this paper than the current discussion suggests. In particular, the manuscript should engage more substantively with the literature it already cites, especially Balland and Rigby’s foundational paper on the geography of complex knowledge (currently reference [15] in the manuscript) and the firm-level application currently listed as reference [18]. In addition, there are recent and directly relevant papers that are not currently integrated into the framing, including work on urban patent-based fitness and complexity [1] and work comparing technological fitness and coherence across geographical scales [2]. More broadly, there is now a substantial adjacent literature using patent data together with complexity-related constructs such as fitness, coherence, relatedness, and spatial concentration, including work on corporate technological portfolios and firm-level technological diversification paths [3]. I therefore suggest substantially revising the literature review and sharpening the novelty claim. The paper should explain more explicitly how it differs from prior patent-complexity studies in terms of unit of analysis, metric, empirical question, and substantive contribution. At present, the manuscript risks overstating novelty and underspecifying its contribution relative to adjacent work. A clearer discussion of overlap and differences with the recent literature on patent complexity, coherent diversification, relatedness, and firm-level technological evolution would help readers understand whether this study provides a genuinely new methodological or empirical contribution, rather than primarily a change in aggregation level and national setting.

2. Interpretation of TCI versus ubiquity is unclear

Section 2.1 needs a much clearer interpretation of what high TCI actually means in this setting. Around lines 130-136, TCI is described as a measure of technological “sophistication,” but the empirical discussion that follows makes it difficult to understand how this interpretation should relate to ubiquity. In Fig. 1C the manuscript reports a positive correlation between ubiquity and TCI, yet several passages are written in ways that suggest that rare technologies should also be the more sophisticated ones. This creates confusion, especially in the discussion of Electrical Engineering versus Chemistry and Pharmaceuticals (roughly lines 185-225). For instance, Electrical Engineering is described as low ubiquity, low average diversity, and low TCI, while Chemistry and Pharmaceuticals is described as rare and high TCI. The reader is left uncertain whether low TCI should be interpreted as low sophistication, low competitiveness, specialization, or something else. I strongly recommend clarifying the conceptual interpretation of TCI and separating it more carefully from rarity and specialization. At present, the text risks conflating three different ideas: rarity of a technology, diversification of the corporations linked to it, and technological sophistication. The manuscript would also benefit from comparing TCI with an alternative complexity metric, such as the Fitness-Complexity framework, because that comparison could make the interpretation more transparent. In a firm-level setting, a technology measure driven by the fitness of the corporations that produce it may be easier to interpret substantively: if a technology is also produced by low-fitness corporations, it should rank as less complex, whereas technologies produced only by high-fitness corporations would rank as more sophisticated.

3. Fig. 1B deserves deeper analysis

The discussion of Fig. 1B is too limited. Around lines 185-190, the manuscript emphasizes the near-zero Pearson correlation between ubiquity and average diversity, but this summary statistic alone does not seem to capture the structure visible in the plot. The figure appears to show a concentration of observations in the high-high and low-low quadrants, with relatively fewer mixed cases. If that visual pattern is real, then the result is more interesting than a simple “negligible correlation” and may indicate non-random assortative structure in the corporation-technology network. I suggest either deepening the analysis of this pattern or toning down the current interpretation. At minimum, the authors should acknowledge that a low global correlation does not rule out structured quadrant concentration. If they want to pursue the point, some measure of spatial association or assortativity would be more informative than a single correlation coefficient.

4. The stability claim in Section 2.2 is too strong for the evidence shown

The manuscript makes a strong claim that the corporate-level approach is more stable or more consistent than the regional-level approach, but the evidence provided in Section 2.2 does not fully support that level of generality. The current argument rests mainly on differences between coarse and fine classifications and on the residual comparison in Fig. 2 (roughly lines 239-321). This is suggestive, but it is not enough to demonstrate broader robustness or stability. In particular, “smaller residuals across classification schemes” is not equivalent to showing that the measure is stable in a stronger methodological sense. I would therefore recommend softening the claim throughout the paper. The current analysis supports the narrower statement that, in this dataset, the corporate-level TCI appears less sensitive than the regional-level TCI to this particular change in classification granularity. Demonstrating a more general stability claim would require complementary evidence, for example sensitivity analyses across time windows, alternative filtering rules, or external benchmarks showing whether technologies expected to remain stable indeed do so.

5. The regional comparison idea is interesting, but currently underdeveloped

The second part of Section 2.2 raises an interesting idea: corporate-level TCI may allow finer regional comparisons than standard regional aggregation. However, the current implementation remains mostly qualitative, while the manuscript draws relatively strong conclusions from it (roughly lines 323-347 and Abstract lines 42-45). The paper shows scatter plots comparing TCI values across prefectures and interprets deviations from the diagonal as differences in regional technological structure, but it does not explain clearly why these deviations should be interpreted as meaningful regional information rather than as noise, instability, or measurement sensitivity. I suggest developing this argument much more carefully or reducing the associated claims. At present, the reader is not told what quantitative criterion should distinguish informative divergence from inconsistency, how strong a difference has to be before it becomes substantively meaningful, how these cross-prefectural differences should be used analytically, or how they could be validated externally. A stronger version of this argument would require some benchmark, similarity metric, or external evidence linking observed TCI differences to known differences in regional production or innovation structures.

6. Several Discussion claims are not fully justified by the Results

Because the Results section remains partly qualitative, the three headline contributions in the Discussion (roughly lines 371-393) currently read as somewhat stronger than the evidence warrants. This is especially true for the second contribution on temporal stability and long-run trajectories, which appears to rely heavily on SI Fig. S2 and on a brief discussion in the main text (roughly lines 309-321). In its current form, Fig. S2 is difficult to read, and it is not sufficient on its own to support a strong methodological claim about stable ranking transitions. I suggest aligning the Discussion more tightly with what is directly demonstrated in the Results. If the authors wish to retain the stronger claims, the main text needs more explicit evidence and clearer exposition. In addition, SI Fig. S2 should either be redesigned to highlight only a small set of trajectories discussed in the text, or replaced with a more interpretable summary. As it stands, the figure is too visually crowded to serve as central support for a main claim.

7. Claims about prediction and policy use are premature

The final part of the Discussion goes beyond what the paper currently demonstrates. Around lines 417-433, the manuscript suggests that the framework can inform predictive modelling and can support strategic or policy-oriented applications. At present, however, the paper does not show how the proposed measure would actually be used in a predictive setting, does not benchmark it against alternative indicators, and does not provide external validation linking it to concrete decision-relevant outcomes. I recommend substantially softening these forward-looking claims. It is reasonable to present prediction and policy use as possible future applications, but not as implications already supported by the present results. In its current form, the paper mainly proposes a corporate-level adaptation of an existing complexity framework; it does not yet demonstrate either a clear predictive advantage or a practically validated policy tool.

8. Corporate identity and firm survival over 1981-2010 need clarification

The data section raises an important longitudinal issue that is currently not addressed. The dataset spans 1981-2010, yet many corporations active at the beginning of the period may no longer exist at the end, and some may have undergone mergers, restructuring, or name changes (roughly lines 439-476). This matters because, at least as the paper currently reads, the analysis appears to treat corporations as stable nodes in the bipartite network, but the manuscript does not explain how corporate identity is harmonized over time. The authors should clarify how they handle firm entry, exit, mergers, and possible changes in assignee names or legal identity. Even a brief explanation would help the reader assess whether the corporation-level network corresponds to stable economic actors or to a more heterogeneous collection of historical assignee records. This point is especially important because the paper later interprets long-run stability in technological rankings despite substantial corporate turnover.

9. The filtering strategy needs stronger justification

The filtering choice in Methods deserves further justification. The manuscript retains only the top 3% of corporations by patent output, but it is not yet clear why this additional ex ante filter is necessary for the complexity analysis. Since the network is constructed after applying the RTA threshold, the authors should first clarify whether the RTA step already removes most low-activity corporations and substantially reduces the sample on its own. A useful robustness check would be to compare the current rule with post-RTA pruning strategies that are more directly motivated by the algorithm. In particular, nodes with only one remaining link are often removed in complexity analyses because they can create numerical instability in ranking. If RTA filtering, possibly combined with the exclusion of degree-one nodes, already reduces the network sufficiently, the top-3% cutoff may not be necessary.

Minor comments

1. Lines 130-136 appear to contain a duplicated definition of TCI in two slightly different versions.

2. The axis labeling in Fig. 1 is not fully consistent. KT,0 and KT,1 are named explicitly, whereas TCI is not labeled in the same style. The label would also read more cleanly without the repeated use of “The (average) ...”.

3. The sentence at lines 371-372 is grammatically incorrect (“patent data identify...”); it likely needs a comma or full restructuring.

4. The abstract is currently too long and too result-specific. It would be stronger if shortened and rewritten in a more standard abstract style, with less space devoted to detailed findings and more emphasis on the core contribution.

5. KT,1 is discussed early in Section 2.1 before its formal derivation appears in the Methods. A brief intuitive definition at first use would improve readability.
